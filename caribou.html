Case Study 01

Mobile Offer Selection Redesign

My task was to redesign Caribou's offer selection experience so customers could easily compare refinance options. No matter what, the redesign needed to boost digital conversion rates.

Before

We knew the original offer screen was designed desktop-first, which made it tough to swipe and compare five possible offers. We wanted research to guide the design we would put into production.

After

The redesigned page was a vertical scroll that gave clear visual cues. Based on research results, we highlighted 'superlatives', like lowest APR, and used a compact card for easy reference across multiple offers.

Mobile Offer Selection

In Depth

Context

In my product role, I advocated to deliver the best possible experience while we had capacity. With engineering, design, and research (me) available, we could show customers the best possible offer (informed by data science and surfaced by engineering), with designs informed by the customers themselves.

Challenge

There are legal requirements that must be met in the fintech sector, which risks dense, intimidating digital experiences. We also had a number of research and design requests from our leadership, given the page's prominence. We needed to balance stakeholder needs, customer needs, and of course, budget and timeline.

Approach

I zeroed in on learning what's most important to customers when they're shopping for a loan, along with evaluating designs for mobile-first. With our lead designer, I conducted live interviews, giving our users Figma prototypes and three design options to click through.

What Changed?

Our users told us they wanted to be able to see a loan's key features right away, not just to save time, but because they hated that feeling they had at car dealerships - like they were being tricked. The design we chose showed key information about an offer, and highlighted 'superlative' offers. Now, it was clear which loan had the lowest APR right away. If it wasn't the number they were looking for, and we had been up front about it.

Case Study 02

New Sales Agent System

I can't provide images of Caribou's internal systems, but I can use silly diagrams and a metaphor or two. The internal system was intended for the power user, the admin, the all-seeing eye. What a system like that is actually doing is not imbuing the user with power. It's actually just throwing all the components of a recipe at you at once, and hoping you get to the intended result. Users with a specific goal, like selling loans, need an actual recipe - a guide that leads you to a successful end result.

Current State: Figure It Out

In one of several silly diagrams, you get an idea of what our Sales team might have to deal with each day, veterans and newbies alike. Sales agents are under immense time pressure to make their numbers, and they had no guidance or guardrails from the tools they relied on.

New State: Workflow, Not a Free-for-All

For the first time, we offered our sales team directions, inside their system, for how to get offers and submit an application. We also showed them where they might need to re-validate information - something the admin-style system didn't do. This was something you just...needed to know. These are the kinds of questions and problems that sales managers handled on Slack all day long.

New Sales Agent System

In Depth

Context

Caribou, like many companies with sales teams, have in-house systems that were meant as a bridge to another system - which was of course never built (or bought). Engineering teams are tasked with these because they have no product, design, or research support, and the system becomes bulky over time with more and more features bolted on. Agents don't complain, they don't request features, and they don't ask for fixes. Instead, they find workarounds and teach them to others. We decided there was a better option, and it would benefit the agent, the customer, and the business.

Challenge

Now that you have the context, you might sense the challenge. There were a lot of bugs and weird old things lurking in the existing system (gremlins, we called them), and staff no longer reported them. At the same time, staff just tended to ask for what already existed, instead of what could be better. Finally, we had gotten used to hearing 'we need this' and 'we have to do that', that the actual necessities in an agent's call with a customer had become obscured over time.

Approach

First, I needed to figure out what the actual workflow was - what did the agent actually do on their calls? What did they do before they spoke with the customer, and then afterwards? What did they absolutely need in their system, and what was 'corporate folklore', as we called it? I also needed to find out, and we already knew quite a bit about this, where our system was failing its users. Where were errors getting through because there was no field-level validation, no alerts that a step needed to be completed before moving on to the next?

What Changed?

Using our recipe metaphor, I pulled out only the essentials and put them into the right order for our agents to follow. Research was constant - multiple rounds before launch, then during to make updates and make this new system was actually useful. Calls with customers became shorter, and delays in getting contracts out to the customer came down.

Research Process: 'Super User' to 'Sales-Only Workflow'

Current State to Ideal State Mapping I conducted interviews, listened to customer calls, and tested our system to learn about the present state, and chart a path forward.
Pre-Launch Testing Once we had our MVP built, I ran two rounds of tests with agents. They conducted tests designed to stress-test the MVP and give feedback based on their daily routines.
Post-Launch Research Once the MVP was launched, I held daily retrospectives with floor managers, gathered anonymous feedback from agents, and reviewed screen recordings and system logs. I created tickets and prioritized fixes with our engineers and operations leadership.
